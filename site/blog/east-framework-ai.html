An EAST Framework for AI Productivity

By Robbie Tilleard · 29 June 2026 · 5 min read

In short: Getting value from AI is a behaviour-change problem, not a tooling problem. Apply EAST (Easy, Attractive, Social, Timely) to your own workflow so the useful action becomes the default one.

The reason AI is not saving you as much time as promised is rarely the model. Far more often it is the friction around it. Getting real value from AI is a behaviour-change problem, and behaviour change has a well-worn practical lens: EAST. Make the useful action Easy, Attractive, Social and Timely, and you'll do it. Leave it effortful and awkward, and you won't, no matter how capable the tool is.

I spent years at a behavioural science outfit watching good interventions fail because they ignored the small stuff: an extra click, a form field, a poorly timed nudge. AI adoption fails the same way. The capability is there. The behaviour around it is badly designed. Here's how to apply each letter to your own AI workflow.

Easy: strip out the friction

Easy is the big one, and it's usually where the time goes to die. Every step between "I could use AI here" and "I have a usable result" is a place to quit. Opening the right tool, finding the last conversation, copying context in, wrangling the output into its final form: each is small, and together they're the difference between a habit and a good intention.

The classic example is fixing AI output. A draft comes back nearly right, and to correct one line you have to re-prompt, or worse, dig through raw HTML to change a word. That friction pushes people into worse behaviour, endless re-prompting instead of a five-second edit. Removing it is why I built htmlEdit, so editing an AI-generated document is as easy as typing on the page. The principle generalises: find the one step you dread and remove it. Defaults do the heavy lifting. If the right tool is already open and pre-loaded with your context, you'll use it. If it isn't, you'll wing it.

Attractive: make the good path obvious

Attractive means making the useful action stand out so you actually notice it's available. Decoration has nothing to do with it. In practice this means saving the prompts that work where you can see them, not buried in a chat history you'll never scroll back through. If your three best prompts are one keystroke away, you'll reach for them. If they're a vague memory, you'll retype something worse.

It also means making outcomes visible. When AI genuinely saves you an hour, notice it. We're wired to repeat what visibly pays off, and a vague sense that "AI helps sometimes" won't reinforce the habit the way a concrete win does.

Social: borrow from people who do it well

You don't have to invent your AI workflow from scratch, and you shouldn't. The fastest way to improve is to copy how good operators actually use these tools: the prompts they reuse, the tasks they delegate, the ones they've learned to keep by hand. Social proof is a strong lever precisely because it's a shortcut. Someone has already run the experiment.

Practically: keep a shared note of prompts that work in your team. Watch how a colleague structures a request. Steal the pattern, adapt it. Most people guard their prompts like trade secrets and reinvent the wheel privately. Sharing them compounds.

Timely: at the moment of need

Timely is about the moment. A tool that's useful but not to hand when the need arises might as well not exist. If capturing a thought, drafting a reply or checking a fact requires switching context and losing your thread, you'll skip it and the moment passes.

The fix is to close the gap between the trigger and the tool. Whatever you reach for most should be reachable in the second you think of it, not after a hunt. This connects to the other letters: Timely is really Easy applied to when rather than how many steps.

Putting it together

You don't need all four perfect. Start with Easy, because friction is the biggest killer, then layer the rest on. Design the setup once so the right behaviour is the default one, and you stop relying on willpower to use AI well. The people getting the most from these tools are the ones who've quietly made the good action the easy one, not the ones with the best prompts. For more on cutting the specific friction of fixing drafts, see Stop Re-Prompting.

Key takeaways

  • Most AI tools fail on adoption, not capability: the useful action is too much effort.
  • Easy: cut the friction between wanting AI's help and getting it.
  • Attractive and Timely: make the good habit obvious and available at the moment of need.
  • Social: borrow prompts and patterns from people doing the work well.
  • Design your setup once so the right behaviour is the path of least resistance.
