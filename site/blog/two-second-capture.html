The Two-Second Rule for Capturing Ideas

By Robbie Tilleard · 1 July 2026 · 4 min read

In short: If capturing an idea takes more than about two seconds, you'll lose most of your ideas. Build a capture system with almost zero friction and sort things later, not in the moment.

Here's the rule: if capturing an idea takes more than about two seconds, you'll lose most of your ideas. Not because they weren't good, and not because your memory is failing. Because the thought arrives, the window is short, and any friction in that window means the idea evaporates before it's written down. Fix the two seconds and you keep the ideas.

Most people treat this as a discipline problem. "I should be better at writing things down." The cause is design, not discipline. The gap between having a thought and logging it is a behavioural bottleneck, and the size of that gap decides how much you keep.

Why ideas are so perishable

An idea in the shower, on a walk, mid-conversation, arrives with almost no warning and leaves just as fast. Working memory is small and easily overwritten. The next thing that grabs your attention, a notification, a question, a bend in the road, and it's gone. You can feel the shape of where a thought was without being able to get it back. Everyone knows the maddening sense of "I had something good and now I don't."

The practical consequence: the moment of capture has to be effortless, because you're competing with everything else clamouring for that sliver of attention. Any step that requires thought, unlocking, opening, choosing where it goes, is a step during which the idea can slip.

The friction is the whole problem

Think about what a slow capture system actually asks of you. Unlock the phone. Find the right app. Decide which notebook or list or project this belongs to. Type. Tag it. Each of those is small. Together they're often more than two seconds, and crucially they demand decisions at exactly the moment your attention is elsewhere.

The most common mistake is trying to organise while capturing. You have a thought, and before you'll write it down you want to know where it lives: is this a work idea, a personal one, which project, which list? That sorting instinct is the enemy. It turns a two-second action into a ten-second one, and ten-second actions don't happen when you're halfway out the door.

Separate capturing from organising

The fix is to split the two jobs. Capturing and organising are different activities with different requirements, and jamming them together ruins both. Capturing needs to be instant and dumb: one place, no decisions, just get the words down. Organising can be slow and thoughtful, because you do it later, at a desk, in a batch, when the cost of a few seconds per item doesn't matter.

So build one capture inbox that's always available and asks nothing of you. A single note. A voice memo. A running list you never have to choose to open. The only rule is that it's the same place every time and reaching it is close to instant. Everything goes there, work and personal and half-formed and brilliant, all mixed together. You'll sort it out later.

This is the EAST idea of Easy applied to your own head. Reduce the steps between intention and action, and the action happens. There's a Timely element too: the tool has to be there in the second you need it, not after a hunt. If capture requires you to first decide anything, you've already lost.

Trust comes from reviewing

A capture system only works if you trust it, and you only trust it if you empty it. An inbox you never review becomes a junk drawer, and once you suspect things go there to die, you stop using it. So pair fast capture with a regular sweep: once a day or once a week, open the inbox, and do the organising you deliberately skipped in the moment. Bin the noise, action the small stuff, file the rest where it belongs.

That review is where the two systems meet. Fast and dumb at capture, slow and deliberate at review. Keep them apart and both work. Merge them and you get a tidy system you never use.

Start with the two seconds

Pick your one capture inbox today and make getting to it as close to instant as you can manage. Don't optimise the organising yet, that comes later and matters less. Just close the gap at the front. The ideas were never the problem. The two seconds were.

Key takeaways

  • Ideas are perishable: the window to capture one closes in seconds.
  • The bottleneck is friction at capture, not memory or discipline.
  • Separate capturing from organising: log now, sort later.
  • One fast, always-available inbox beats several tidy but slow systems.
  • Review your capture inbox on a schedule so it stays trusted.
