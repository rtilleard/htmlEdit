Small Tools, Big Leverage: The Case for Single-Purpose Apps

By Robbie Tilleard · 10 July 2026 · 4 min read

In short: A tool that does one thing well beats a bloated platform that does ten things adequately. Small single-purpose apps are easier to learn, faster to use, and harder to get wrong.

If you have a specific, annoying job to do, a small tool built for exactly that job will almost always beat a sprawling platform that lists it as feature number thirty-seven. The single-purpose app has one thing to get right, so it usually gets it right. The all-in-one suite has a thousand things to get roughly right, and roughly right is where most of them live.

We have been trained to want the opposite. Bigger feels safer. One login, one subscription, one vendor, one place for everything. But the everything-app promise has a catch that shows up the moment you actually try to do the one thing you came for.

Why big tools feel heavy

A platform that does ten jobs has to make ten compromises. Every screen has to accommodate ten workflows, so every button is a little further from where you need it. The onboarding has to teach ten things, so it teaches all of them shallowly. The settings menu is enormous because it has to be. You spend your first hour learning the tool instead of doing the work.

There is a behavioural point buried in here. Big tools are often designed to win a feature comparison, not to make your task fast. A checklist of two hundred features sells the deal. But you do not use two hundred features. You use three, badly served, surrounded by the other one hundred and ninety-seven you are paying for and stepping around.

A small tool has none of that weight. It opens, it does the thing, you leave. The learning curve is a step, not a hill. There is less to break, less to update, less to hold in your head.

Small tools compose

The real magic of single-purpose tools is that they combine. This is the old idea behind the command line: lots of little programs that each do one thing, piped together however the moment requires. You are not locked into one vendor's idea of your workflow. You assemble your own.

The AI era makes this more relevant, not less. A model might draft your document, another tool might turn it into HTML, and then you need one specific thing: to fix the rendered result without wrestling raw code. That is a job for a tool that does only that. htmlEdit is deliberately narrow: it lets you edit an AI-generated HTML document visually, by clicking and typing on the page, and nothing else. It does not want to be your email client, your CRM, or your operating system. It does the one step the big tools skip.

That narrowness is the feature. Because it only does one thing, it can slot into whatever chain you have without asking you to move your whole life into it.

Ownership beats renting

Small, focused tools also tend to respect you more. There is less incentive to trap you, because the pitch is competence, not lock-in. Many are open source, run locally, and keep your data on your machine. You are using a tool, not moving into a walled garden and paying rent on your own work.

Compare that to the platform that owns your files, your history and your habits, and quietly raises the price once you cannot leave. The switching cost is the business model. With a small tool, if something better comes along, you just use the better thing. Nothing is holding you hostage.

None of this means every job wants a boutique tool. Some things genuinely benefit from integration, and there is a real cost to juggling twenty logins. But the default assumption, that bigger and more unified is automatically better, deserves more suspicion than it gets. For most specific, recurring annoyances, the right answer is a small sharp tool that does that one thing and gets out of your way.

The next time you reach for the giant platform to solve a narrow problem, ask what you actually need. Often it is one clean step. And the tool that does only that step will beat the one that does everything.

Key takeaways

  • Single-purpose tools have a shallower learning curve and less to go wrong.
  • All-in-one platforms optimise for feature checklists, not for your task.
  • Small tools compose: you can chain them however you like.
  • You keep control and ownership instead of renting a walled garden.
  • The best tool often just does the one annoying step nobody else covers.
